Policy Brief: Strengthening Pesticide Poisoning Surveillance

Five Short-Term Options for Improving Detection and Response

ImportantFor Decision-Makers

Target audience: Ministerial Advisory Committee (MAC), National Department of Health (NDoH), Provincial Health Leadership

Reading time: 8 minutes

Key message: South Africa has the legal authority to strengthen pesticide poisoning surveillance — we just need to execute better. Five low-cost options can improve detection by 50–85% without new legislation. The economic case is overwhelming: R0.24M–R1.19M/year of surveillance spend offsets ~R260M/year of avoided burden cost against a total burden of ~R2.3B/year.

TipD2P Recommendation

Adopt Options 3a + 3b + 4 as the Minimum Viable Package; scale to the Full Package within 18 months.

  • Pilot: 6 months, 3 provinces (Gauteng, Western Cape, KZN), ~R950,000 total
  • Lead agency: NICD as national coordinator
  • Quick wins (weeks 1–4): Option 3a (BChE IT activation under existing MOU) + Option 4 (PIH live dashboard)
  • Medium term (months 3–12): Option 3b (toxicology auto-notify — build new) + Option 2 (NICD→DALRRD feed) + Option 4 MVD integration into NMC
  • Evaluate Q1 2027 against avoided-burden ratio; scale nationally if it holds

The Problem in Plain Language

Three independent surveillance systems each capture part of the picture but never share records:

  • NMC notifications: ~690 cases/year
  • NHLS severe-poisoning laboratory results: ~2,000 cases/year
  • Poisons Information Helpline (PIH): ~9,000 pesticide-related calls/year

In the absence of record linkage we treat these sources as mutually exclusive, giving a true annual burden of ~11,690 cases. Applying the NMC-observed case-fatality rate of 10–15% yields ~1,500–1,800 deaths/year (the headline uses upper-mid CFR 13–15%; vital-registration data systematically under-codes intentional self-poisoning under ICD-10 default rules, so this is conservative — see the VR poisoning-deaths analysis). Monetised using a per-case morbidity cost of ~R12,000 and a human-capital mortality cost of ~R1.5M, the total economic burden is ~R2.3 billion/year.

Most victims are:

  • Young children (accidental exposure from unsafe storage)
  • Adolescent girls (intentional self-harm)
  • Adult male farm workers (occupational exposure)

Why does this matter?

  • Public health risk: Clusters of poisonings may signal contaminated food, illegal pesticide sales, or unsafe working conditions — but we detect them too late (or not at all)
  • Preventable deaths: Early detection of hotspots could trigger rapid response (source removal, environmental sampling, community warnings)
  • Wasted resources: Three government agencies collect pesticide data independently, but they don’t talk to each other

What’s Causing the Failure?

Status Quo: A One-Way, Broken Pipeline

Notifications today travel in one direction only, and the chain breaks before it reaches the regulator who can act on it:

What this shows:

  • Clinicians and public-health officials do notify on the NMC platform — that is the formal entry point. The NMC platform then sends an SMS to the local EHP, who is mandated to investigate.
  • EHPs do not update NMC with the outcome of their investigation. There is no field, no workflow, and no requirement to do so.
  • EHPs may informally refer the matter to DALRRD (phone, email, personal contact). This referral is not recorded on NMC either.
  • Nothing closes the loop back to NMC or the clinician. DALRRD may give feedback to the EHP at best — but the clinician, NICD, and the surveillance dashboard never learn what (if anything) happened.

Coordination Decision Tree: Where the Chain Breaks

The same pathway, viewed as a sequence of decisions, makes it obvious where coordination fails by default — not because anyone refuses to act, but because no decision node has a mandatory “record it on NMC” branch.

Reading the tree:

  • There are eight decision nodes between “case suspected” and “loop fully closed”. A case must clear all eight for coordination to actually work.
  • Six of the eight have a default branch that exits to a red (loop-broken) terminal — usually because no system requires the actor to record what they did.
  • The only two green exits (G1, G2) depend on behaviours that are not currently mandated: EHPs writing investigation outcomes onto NMC, and DALRRD feeding regulatory action back to NMC and the clinician.
  • This is why the policy options below all target a specific decision node: Option 1 (NHLS→NMC IT feed) replaces the fragile Q1 decision with an automatic one; Option 3a/3b do the same for laboratory-confirmed cases; Option 2 (NICD→DALRRD) and the Full Package feedback loop force Q5, Q6, and Q7 to become recorded, structured steps.

A fishbone analysis identified five root causes — all fixable without new laws:

Five Policy Options (All Legally Feasible Today)

Status Quo: Keep Doing What We’re Doing

What it is:

  • Clinicians manually file NMC notifications (often 2–4 weeks late)
  • NHLS runs ~9,000 BChE tests/year but results don’t trigger public health alerts
  • PIH answers ~9,000 calls/year but data aren’t analyzed for surveillance

Performance:

  • Detects ~4.5 of 10 annual hotspots
  • Annual cost: R0 (no change)

Bottom line: Cheap, but ineffective.

Option 1: Activate the NHLS–NMC Data Feed (IT Implementation)

What it is:

  • An NHLS–NICD MOU for routine BChE data sharing already exists on paper. What is missing is the IT implementation — a working LIS-to-NMC pipe.
  • Stand up the technical connector (HL7 / flat-file export from NHLS LIS → NMC ingest), governance, and a monthly QA review.
  • De-identified, aggregated BChE data flows into NMC for trend analysis.

Legal basis: Existing MOU under Section 90 of the National Health Act (2003). No new legal instrument required.

Performance:

  • Detects ~5.2 of 10 annual hotspots
  • Annual cost: ~R40,000 ongoing (QA + liaison); one-off IT setup ~R80,000
  • Implementation time: 8–12 weeks

Bottom line: The cheapest fix on the menu — the agreement exists, the data exists, only the IT plumbing is missing.

Option 2: NICD→DALRRD Poisoning Intelligence Feed

What it is:

  • DALRRD has formally requested the poisoning intelligence held by NICD/NMC so that pesticide registration, labelling, and inspectorate priorities can be informed by real-world morbidity and mortality.
  • The flow is NICD/NMC → DALRRD (poisoning cases by suspected product, geography, season), not the other way round.
  • Output: a quarterly NICD→DALRRD brief plus an ad-hoc cluster alert channel.

Legal basis: Inter-departmental data sharing protocols; de-identified aggregate data.

Performance:

  • Detects ~5.8 of 10 annual hotspots (via downstream regulatory action reducing supply)
  • Annual cost: ~R40,000 (NICD analyst time + DALRRD liaison)
  • Implementation time: 12 weeks

Bottom line: Closes the regulator-feedback gap. DALRRD is asking for this — the cost of not sending it is regulatory blindness.

Option 3a: BChE Auto-Notification — Capability Exists (★ Recommended)

What it is:

  • Configure the existing NHLS Laboratory Information System (LIS) to automatically notify NICD when BChE results show severe poisoning (< 50% of normal).
  • Notification within 24 hours of result validation; no clinician action required.
  • The laboratory capability already exists — NHLS performs ~9,000 BChE tests/year. Only the auto-notification rule and message handler need to be configured.

Legal basis: Already permitted under NMC regulations; requires only IT configuration.

Performance:

  • Detects ~6.7 of 10 annual hotspots
  • Annual cost: ~R55,000 (IT staff time)
  • Implementation time: 2–4 weeks

Why this is a game-changer:

  • Fast: cuts notification delay from 2–4 weeks to <24 hours
  • Automated: removes reliance on clinicians filing paperwork
  • Building on existing capability: no new lab test, no new reagent contract

Bottom line: Best value for money. Recommended first priority.

Option 3b: Chemical Toxicology Auto-Notification — New Capability (★ Recommended, medium term)

What it is:

  • Extend auto-notification to chemical toxicology results (qualitative/quantitative pesticide assays).
  • This capability does not yet exist on live patients — NHLS currently performs chemical toxicology post-mortem only (see Surveillance Concept Note — Component 6).
  • Phase 1: stand up live-patient toxicology at a sentinel tertiary site (CHB). Phase 2–3: expand to 4–5 sites with auto-notification wired in.

Legal basis: NMC regulations permit lab-triggered notification; no new instrument required.

Performance:

  • Detects an additional ~10–15 percentage points on top of Option 3a, by capturing non-cholinesterase agents (pyrethroids, phosphides, paraquat, carbamates at high doses) and confirming the specific compound.
  • Annual cost: ~R1.2M (pilot site) scaling to ~R3.6M (5-site full rollout) — funded as research-surveillance investment.
  • Implementation time: 6–12 months for pilot site build.

Why this matters:

  • Agent attribution. Option 3a tells us a poisoning happened; Option 3b tells us which product caused it — the prerequisite for targeted DALRRD action under Option 2.
  • Survivor evidence base. ~10,000 survivors/year currently have agent recorded as “suspected/unknown”.

Bottom line: Higher cost, but the only route to product-specific regulation. Funded via research streams (SAMRC, Bloomberg, Gates), not the surveillance budget.

Option 4: PIH Minimum Viable Dataset + Live Dashboard (★ Recommended)

What it is:

  • Short term (weeks 1–2): Poisons Information Helpline (PIH) publishes a live dashboard of pesticide-related calls (counts, geography, suspected agent, severity, demographics) updated daily/weekly.
  • Medium term (months 3–9): Agree a minimum viable dataset (MVD) between PIH and NICD so that PIH call records can be ingested into NMC as a routine, structured data stream.
  • Currently, PIH receives ~9,000 pesticide-related calls/year with rich clinical context but no agreed data spec for surveillance use.

Legal basis: Existing PIH–NICD MOU.

Performance:

  • Detects ~6.1 of 10 annual hotspots (rises further once MVD integration is live)
  • Annual cost: ~R62,000 (PIH analyst time + dashboard hosting)
  • Implementation time: 1–2 weeks for dashboard; 3–9 months for MVD/NMC integration

Why this is valuable:

  • Dashboard ships in days, not months — immediate visibility for NICD and provinces while the MVD is negotiated.
  • Complements BChE data: PIH captures cases that never reach a laboratory.
  • Clinical context: PIH records suspected product, route, intent, and time-to-call — fields a BChE result cannot provide.

Bottom line: Lowest-hanging fruit. Recommended joint first priority with Option 3a.

NoteWhy is there no “Option 5” any more?

Earlier drafts bundled provincial response coordination into the surveillance package. The current framing separates the two:

  • Options 1–4 are the surveillance-improvement layer (data flows, detection, agent attribution).
  • Response, coordination, and field investigation are handled in the parallel coordination-mechanism workstream (NICD outbreak response, provincial DoH, EHP/DALRRD inspectorate) and budgeted separately in the Costing Analysis.

This avoids double-counting and lets each workstream be commissioned, funded, and evaluated on its own terms.

Full Package: All Surveillance Options Combined

What it is:

  • Combine Options 1, 2, 3a, 3b, and 4 into an integrated surveillance system (response coordination is delivered separately).

What changes vs. the status quo:

  • Two-way flow. Every solid arrow into NICD is matched by a dotted feedback arrow back out.
  • EHP → DALRRD becomes structured, with a written referral and a logged enforcement outcome.
  • Clinicians close the loop: they learn whether the case they notified contributed to a cluster and what regulatory action followed.

Performance:

  • Detects ~8.3 of 10 annual hotspots
  • Annual cost: ~R237,000 (< R20,000/month)
  • Implementation time: 12 weeks

Why this is the best choice:

  • 85% effectiveness: Dramatically better than status quo (45%)
  • Redundancy: Multiple data sources cross-validate each other
  • Affordable: Less than the cost of one full-time epidemiologist

Bottom line: If budget allows, this is the gold standard.

Sensitivity Analysis: What If We’re Wrong?

All cost and effectiveness estimates are based on placeholder assumptions (the model will be updated with real data from the pilot). But even under pessimistic scenarios, the recommendations hold:

Pessimistic Scenario (Low Effectiveness, High Cost)

Assume:

  • Options are only 50% as effective as base-case estimates
  • Costs are 2× higher than budgeted

Result:

  • Option 3 (BChE auto-notify) still improves detection by +12% for ~R110,000/year
  • Full Package still improves detection by +29% for ~R474,000/year

Conclusion: Even if we’re substantially wrong, the options remain cost-effective.

Optimistic Scenario (High Effectiveness, Low Cost)

Assume:

  • Options are 150% as effective as base-case estimates
  • Costs are 50% lower than budgeted

Result:

  • Option 3 improves detection by +86% for ~R28,000/year
  • Full Package achieves near-perfect detection (9.5 / 10 hotspots) for ~R119,000/year

Conclusion: There’s significant upside potential if implementation goes smoothly.

Decision Framework for MAC/NDoH

TipThree Decision Paths

Path 1: Minimal Investment, Maximum Speed

Recommendation: Stand up the Option 4 PIH live dashboard immediately

Why:

  • Can be launched in 1–2 weeks
  • Costs only ~R62,000/year
  • No new IT infrastructure or legal review
  • Improves detection by +36%

Who should choose this: Provinces or sponsors with limited budget or IT capacity

Path 2: Best Value for Money

Recommendation: Implement Option 3a (BChE auto-notify) — capability already exists, only IT config needed

Why:

  • Best effectiveness per rand spent (+49% for R55,000/year)
  • Fully automated once configured
  • Operational in 2–4 weeks

Who should choose this: Provinces with NHLS IT support available

Path 3: Comprehensive Surveillance Solution

Recommendation: Implement Full Surveillance Package (Options 1 + 2 + 3a + 4)

Why:

  • Achieves ~80% effectiveness (vs. 45% status quo)
  • Multiple data sources cross-validate; DALRRD closes the regulatory loop
  • Still affordable (~R197,000/year ongoing)
  • Option 3b (sentinel toxicology) added later via research funding

Who should choose this: National rollout; provinces with adequate budget

Legal and Ethical Considerations

Is This Legal?

Yes. All options can be implemented under existing law:

  • Section 90 of the National Health Act (2003): Permits inter-agency data sharing for public health purposes
  • NMC Regulations: Allow laboratory-triggered notifications (Option 3)
  • Existing MOUs: PIH-NICD collaboration already established (Option 4)

No new legislation required.

What About Privacy?

  • All data sharing follows POPIA (Protection of Personal Information Act) requirements
  • Laboratory auto-notifications include only:
    • Patient demographics (age, sex, facility)
    • BChE value
    • Test date
  • No names, ID numbers, or contact details are shared
  • De-identification protocols apply to all aggregated reports

What If We Do Nothing?

Risks of maintaining the status quo:

  1. Ongoing under-detection of hotspots → preventable deaths continue
  2. Wasted data: NHLS and PIH collect valuable information that goes unused
  3. Missed clusters: Contaminated food sources, illegal pesticide sales, or occupational hazards remain undetected
  4. International embarrassment: South Africa’s surveillance system lags behind regional peers (Botswana, Kenya have better pesticide monitoring)

Next Steps

For National Leadership (MAC, NDoH)

  1. Review this brief and the full decision analysis report
  2. Select a preferred option (or Full Package)
  3. Approve pilot funding (~R120,000 for 6-month pilot in 2–3 provinces)
  4. Designate a lead agency (recommend NICD as national coordinator)

For Provincial Leadership

  1. Identify a surveillance focal point (existing epidemiologist or environmental health officer)
  2. Assess local NHLS capacity for BChE auto-notification (Option 3)
  3. Allocate budget for pilot participation (~R40,000–R80,000 depending on options selected)

For NICD/NHLS/PIH

  1. Finalize MOUs for data sharing (Months 1–2)
  2. Configure auto-notification in pilot provinces (Months 2–3)
  3. Launch pilot (Month 3)
  4. Monitor and evaluate (Months 3–6)
  5. Report findings to MAC (Month 6)

Timeline

Conclusion

South Africa’s pesticide poisoning surveillance system is failing — but we can fix it quickly and affordably. The legal authority exists, the data sources exist, and the technology exists. What’s missing is coordination and execution.

Our recommendation:

  1. Start small: Pilot Options 3 and 4 in 2–3 provinces (total cost: ~R120,000 for 6 months)
  2. Measure impact: Track timeliness, completeness, and hotspot detection rates
  3. Scale up: Expand to national rollout if pilot succeeds

The cost of inaction — preventable deaths, missed outbreaks, and wasted data — far exceeds the cost of these simple improvements.

### Option 1: Activate the NHLS–NMC Data Feed (IT Implementation)

**What it is:**

- An **NHLS–NICD MOU for routine BChE data sharing already exists** on paper. What is missing is the **IT implementation** — a working LIS-to-NMC pipe.
- Stand up the technical connector (HL7 / flat-file export from NHLS LIS → NMC ingest), governance, and a monthly QA review.
- De-identified, aggregated BChE data flows into NMC for trend analysis.

**Legal basis:** Existing MOU under Section 90 of the National Health Act (2003). **No new legal instrument required.**

**Performance:**

- Detects ~5.2 of 10 annual hotspots
- Annual cost: ~R40,000 ongoing (QA + liaison); one-off IT setup ~R80,000
- Implementation time: 8–12 weeks

**Bottom line:** The cheapest fix on the menu — the agreement exists, the data exists, only the IT plumbing is missing.

---

### Option 2: NICD→DALRRD Poisoning Intelligence Feed

**What it is:**

- **DALRRD has formally requested** the poisoning intelligence held by NICD/NMC so that pesticide registration, labelling, and inspectorate priorities can be informed by real-world morbidity and mortality.
- The flow is **NICD/NMC → DALRRD** (poisoning cases by suspected product, geography, season), not the other way round.
- Output: a quarterly NICD→DALRRD brief plus an ad-hoc cluster alert channel.

**Legal basis:** Inter-departmental data sharing protocols; de-identified aggregate data.

**Performance:**

- Detects ~5.8 of 10 annual hotspots (via downstream regulatory action reducing supply)
- Annual cost: ~R40,000 (NICD analyst time + DALRRD liaison)
- Implementation time: 12 weeks

**Bottom line:** Closes the regulator-feedback gap. DALRRD is asking for this — the cost of *not* sending it is regulatory blindness.

---

### Option 3a: BChE Auto-Notification — *Capability Exists* (★ Recommended)

**What it is:**

- Configure the existing NHLS Laboratory Information System (LIS) to automatically notify NICD when **BChE results show severe poisoning** (< 50% of normal).
- Notification within **24 hours** of result validation; no clinician action required.
- **The laboratory capability already exists** — NHLS performs ~9,000 BChE tests/year. Only the auto-notification rule and message handler need to be configured.

**Legal basis:** Already permitted under NMC regulations; requires only IT configuration.

**Performance:**

- Detects ~6.7 of 10 annual hotspots
- Annual cost: ~R55,000 (IT staff time)
- Implementation time: **2–4 weeks**

**Why this is a game-changer:**

- **Fast:** cuts notification delay from 2–4 weeks to <24 hours
- **Automated:** removes reliance on clinicians filing paperwork
- **Building on existing capability:** no new lab test, no new reagent contract

**Bottom line:** Best value for money. **Recommended first priority.**

---

### Option 3b: Chemical Toxicology Auto-Notification — *New Capability* (★ Recommended, medium term)

**What it is:**

- Extend auto-notification to **chemical toxicology results** (qualitative/quantitative pesticide assays).
- **This capability does not yet exist on live patients** — NHLS currently performs chemical toxicology *post-mortem only* (see [Surveillance Concept Note — Component 6](surveillance_concept_note.qmd#sec-component-6)).
- Phase 1: stand up live-patient toxicology at a sentinel tertiary site (CHB). Phase 2–3: expand to 4–5 sites with auto-notification wired in.

**Legal basis:** NMC regulations permit lab-triggered notification; no new instrument required.

**Performance:**

- Detects an additional ~10–15 percentage points on top of Option 3a, by capturing non-cholinesterase agents (pyrethroids, phosphides, paraquat, carbamates at high doses) and confirming the specific compound.
- Annual cost: ~R1.2M (pilot site) scaling to ~R3.6M (5-site full rollout) — funded as research-surveillance investment.
- Implementation time: 6–12 months for pilot site build.

**Why this matters:**

- **Agent attribution.** Option 3a tells us a poisoning happened; Option 3b tells us *which product caused it* — the prerequisite for targeted DALRRD action under Option 2.
- **Survivor evidence base.** ~10,000 survivors/year currently have agent recorded as "suspected/unknown".

**Bottom line:** Higher cost, but the only route to product-specific regulation. Funded via research streams (SAMRC, Bloomberg, Gates), not the surveillance budget.

---

### Option 4: PIH Minimum Viable Dataset + Live Dashboard (★ Recommended)

**What it is:**

- **Short term (weeks 1–2):** Poisons Information Helpline (PIH) publishes a **live dashboard** of pesticide-related calls (counts, geography, suspected agent, severity, demographics) updated daily/weekly.
- **Medium term (months 3–9):** Agree a **minimum viable dataset (MVD)** between PIH and NICD so that PIH call records can be ingested into NMC as a routine, structured data stream.
- Currently, PIH receives ~9,000 pesticide-related calls/year with rich clinical context but no agreed data spec for surveillance use.

**Legal basis:** Existing PIH–NICD MOU.

**Performance:**

- Detects ~6.1 of 10 annual hotspots (rises further once MVD integration is live)
- Annual cost: ~R62,000 (PIH analyst time + dashboard hosting)
- Implementation time: **1–2 weeks** for dashboard; 3–9 months for MVD/NMC integration

**Why this is valuable:**

- **Dashboard ships in days, not months** — immediate visibility for NICD and provinces while the MVD is negotiated.
- **Complements BChE data:** PIH captures cases that never reach a laboratory.
- **Clinical context:** PIH records suspected product, route, intent, and time-to-call — fields a BChE result cannot provide.

**Bottom line:** Lowest-hanging fruit. **Recommended joint first priority with Option 3a.**

---

::: {.callout-note}
## Why is there no "Option 5" any more?

Earlier drafts bundled **provincial response coordination** into the surveillance package. The current framing separates the two:

- **Options 1–4** are the **surveillance-improvement** layer (data flows, detection, agent attribution).
- **Response, coordination, and field investigation** are handled in the parallel coordination-mechanism workstream (NICD outbreak response, provincial DoH, EHP/DALRRD inspectorate) and budgeted separately in the [Costing Analysis](costing_analysis.qmd).

This avoids double-counting and lets each workstream be commissioned, funded, and evaluated on its own terms.
:::

### Full Package: All Surveillance Options Combined

**What it is:**

- Combine Options 1, 2, 3a, 3b, and 4 into an **integrated surveillance system** (response coordination is delivered separately).

- Detects ~8.3 of 10 annual hotspots
- Annual cost: ~R237,000 (< R20,000/month)
- Implementation time: 12 weeks

- **Redundancy:** Multiple data sources cross-validate each other
- **Affordable:** Less than the cost of one full-time epidemiologist

**Bottom line:** If budget allows, this is the **gold standard**.

## Sensitivity Analysis: What If We're Wrong?

All cost and effectiveness estimates are based on **placeholder assumptions** (the model will be updated with real data from the pilot). But even under pessimistic scenarios, the recommendations hold:

Assume:

- Options are only 50% as effective as base-case estimates
- Costs are 2× higher than budgeted

**Result:**

- Option 3 (BChE auto-notify) still improves detection by +12% for ~R110,000/year
- Full Package still improves detection by +29% for ~R474,000/year

**Conclusion:** Even if we're substantially wrong, the options remain cost-effective.

### Optimistic Scenario (High Effectiveness, Low Cost)

Assume:

- Options are 150% as effective as base-case estimates
- Costs are 50% lower than budgeted

**Result:**

- Option 3 improves detection by +86% for ~R28,000/year
- Full Package achieves near-perfect detection (9.5 / 10 hotspots) for ~R119,000/year

**Conclusion:** There's significant upside potential if implementation goes smoothly.

## Legal and Ethical Considerations

### Is This Legal?

**Yes.** All options can be implemented under existing law:

- **Section 90 of the National Health Act (2003):** Permits inter-agency data sharing for public health purposes
- **NMC Regulations:** Allow laboratory-triggered notifications (Option 3)
- **Existing MOUs:** PIH-NICD collaboration already established (Option 4)

**No new legislation required.**

### What About Privacy?

- All data sharing follows **POPIA (Protection of Personal Information Act)** requirements
- Laboratory auto-notifications include only:
  - Patient demographics (age, sex, facility)
  - BChE value
  - Test date
- **No names, ID numbers, or contact details** are shared
- De-identification protocols apply to all aggregated reports

### What If We Do Nothing?

**Risks of maintaining the status quo:**

1. **Ongoing under-detection** of hotspots → preventable deaths continue
2. **Wasted data:** NHLS and PIH collect valuable information that goes unused
3. **Missed clusters:** Contaminated food sources, illegal pesticide sales, or occupational hazards remain undetected
4. **International embarrassment:** South Africa's surveillance system lags behind regional peers (Botswana, Kenya have better pesticide monitoring)

## Conclusion

South Africa's pesticide poisoning surveillance system is failing — but **we can fix it quickly and affordably**. The legal authority exists, the data sources exist, and the technology exists. What's missing is **coordination and execution**.

**Our recommendation:**

1. **Start small:** Pilot Options 3 and 4 in 2–3 provinces (total cost: ~R120,000 for 6 months)
2. **Measure impact:** Track timeliness, completeness, and hotspot detection rates
3. **Scale up:** Expand to national rollout if pilot succeeds

**The cost of inaction** — preventable deaths, missed outbreaks, and wasted data — far exceeds the cost of these simple improvements.
