The other quantitative inputs — market volume, yield impact, substitute hazard, substitute pricing, enforcement cost — are not epistemic gaps: they are either administrative numbers DALRRD can pull, or values defensibly anchored to the EU/US/Sri Lanka evidence base (see §2b and Interim assumptions).

1. Policy question

Should South Africa restrict the use of terbufos, or ban it outright as a registered agricultural pesticide?

2. Public health problem

Terbufos is a WHO Class Ia (“Extremely Hazardous”) organophosphate insecticide.1 It is acutely toxic via oral, dermal, and inhalation routes, with a published rat oral LD50 of 1.6–4.5 mg/kg23 — placing it among the most acutely toxic active ingredients still in commercial use anywhere. It acts via irreversible cholinesterase inhibition; sub-gram doses are lethal in adults and very small quantities are lethal in children.

South African evidence base. Davies et al. (2023), reviewing paediatric pesticide deaths in South Africa, identified terbufos and methamidophos as the dominant agents in fatal childhood pesticide poisoning, with most deaths occurring after ingestion of granular formulations diverted into the informal “street-pesticide” market.4 This is corroborated by NICD/NMC clinical case reports and by the public-health record of mass-poisoning events in 2022–2024 linked to informally-sold granular OP pesticides.5 A formal case series with attributed agent breakdown across NMC + NHLS + forensic toxicology has not yet been compiled and is the single most important evidence-gap item to retire (see open items in the Parameters Hub, TBF-2).

The public-health case rests on three claims:

  1. High intrinsic toxicity at sub-gram doses — settled science (WHO Class Ia; rat oral LD50 1.6–4.5 mg/kg).67
  2. Disproportionate contribution to severe and fatal poisonings — supported in paediatric SA data (Davies 2023);8 adult attributable fraction is not yet quantified pending live-patient chemical toxicology (Surveillance Option S3).
  3. High substitutability for its primary use cases (soil insect / stalk borer / nematode control in maize and other field crops) — registered substitutes exist (see §5), but SA-specific yield-impact modelling is not yet done (open item TBF-3).

2b. International natural-experiment evidence

South Africa does not have to model terbufos withdrawal from first principles. Three relevant natural experiments have already been run:

  • Sri Lanka (1995–2011). Sequential bans on WHO Class I organophosphates reduced pesticide-suicide mortality by ~50 % and total suicide mortality by ~21 %, without compensating method substitution.9 Each banned single agent typically accounted for 20–40 % of fatal pesticide poisonings in the period immediately before its withdrawal.
  • Bangladesh, Nepal, India. Class Ia/Ib restrictions show the same direction and order of magnitude.
  • EU (2009) and US EPA (2006). Terbufos was withdrawn (EU) or moved to RUP-only with granular formulations voluntarily cancelled (US). No documented maize-yield collapse in any producer state.

The Sri Lanka studies are the strongest available evidence that single-agent Class Ia withdrawal does retire most of that agent’s attributable fatal burden, and does not redistribute it to substitute agents at the same lethality. SA paediatric data (Davies 2023) is independently consistent with terbufos sitting in the 20–40 % attributable bracket.

Probabilistic sensitivity analysis (PSA). Each CSV row carries a dist=<family>(<params>) tag (beta, gamma, fixed) parsed by the analysis pipeline (wrangling_v2.r) for Monte Carlo PSA. Distributions follow standard health-economics conventions: gamma for costs (shape 25 → CV ≈ 0.20), beta for probabilities (parameterised on the sample-equivalent count), fixed for deterministic anchors and derived sums.

What this site does not do. No mixed-effects regression, no machine-learning model, no causal-inference identification strategy, no Bayesian hierarchical model. The site is a transparent deterministic burden-and-decision-tree calculator with explicit PSA. Strength comes from auditability, not algorithmic complexity.

NoteWhat DALRRD action delivers — and to whom

DALRRD itself bears ~R0 of the R2.34B annual burden. That cost is carried by NDoH (~R70M hospital), NHLS (~R10M lab), and most heavily by households (~R2.26B in out-of-pocket costs and lost lifetime earnings). DALRRD nevertheless holds the only lever — registration, restriction, recall — that prevents the underlying exposure in the first place. A regulatory action borne by DALRRD (~R1–3M/year of additional enforcement and inspectorate capacity) therefore retires household-borne mortality cost at a leverage ratio of roughly 1 : 500: one rand of regulatory spend averts five hundred rand of household income loss. For DALRRD this is not a cost — it is the single highest-impact use of regulatory authority on the SADC pesticide-poisoning portfolio.

Why act now?

  • 2022–2024 mass-poisoning events placed terbufos in the public and political spotlight,14 creating a window for regulatory action that may not recur.
  • The EU removed terbufos from its approved list more than a decade ago; South Africa is an outlier among middle-income agricultural exporters in still permitting it.
  • The Surveillance + Coordination investments give DALRRD, for the first time, the institutional pathway to act on a single-product signal.

4. Root cause analysis

The drivers cluster into intrinsic-hazard, formulation, market, attribution-evidence, and substitution-readiness causes. Three of five are addressed by regulatory action on terbufos itself; R4 by the Surveillance brief; R5 by DALRRD–grain industry co-design.

5. Policy options

Option T1 — No change (status quo)

Terbufos remains registered under existing conditions. Relies on general pesticide-handling regulations and clinician/EHP-led NMC notifications to detect harm. This is the implicit decision if no regulatory action is taken.

Option T2 — Tighter restriction (★ recommended interim)

A bundle of measures short of an outright ban:

  • Formulation restriction. Withdraw granular formulations that visually resemble food/seed; allow only encapsulated or liquid formulations dispensed via licensed agricultural distributors.
  • Restricted-use product (RUP) designation. Sales only to certified applicators against a logged transaction; no informal-market sales.
  • Mandatory point-of-sale labelling and lockable container requirement.
  • Quarterly DALRRD audit of distributor records, with NICD→DALRRD intelligence feed (see Coordination brief Option C3) flagging geographies of concern.
  • Sunset clause. Conditional 24-month re-registration; if NICD-attributed serious-poisoning cases (from S3 toxicology, once operational) do not decline by a pre-agreed margin, automatic transition to Option T3.

Cost: principally regulatory and enforcement. [Evidence gap: DALRRD inspectorate cost model for RUP enforcement.] Implementation: 6–12 months.

Option T3 — Full ban / phase-out

Cancel terbufos product registrations under the Fertilizers, Farm Feeds, Agricultural Remedies and Stock Remedies Act. Phase-out window (12–24 months) to allow substitute uptake and product clearance. Aligns South Africa with the EU position.

Trade-offs to manage:

  • Crop-yield impact on maize. [Evidence gap: SA-specific yield modelling under leading substitute actives; requires DALRRD + grain-industry collaboration.]
  • Substitute toxicity profile. Some substitutes (e.g. fipronil, certain neonicotinoids) carry their own ecological and human-health concerns. [Evidence gap: SA-specific comparative hazard assessment.]
  • Smallholder access. Substitutes may be more expensive or harder to access at smallholder scale. [Evidence gap: substitute pricing and accessibility.]
  • Compliance / black-market risk. A poorly-enforced ban can drive demand to unregulated supply. Mitigated by DALRRD inspectorate capacity (Coordination C3) and by Option T2 acting as a 24-month stepping stone.

Cost: regulatory cost low; market-transition cost concentrated on growers and distributors. [Evidence gap.] Implementation: 12–24 months.

6. Decision tree — terbufos regulatory choices

How to read the tree. Public-health, agricultural, and implementation consequences are shown separately rather than collapsed into a single utility. T1 is best for agriculture and implementation but worst for public health. T3 is best for public health but loads risk onto the agricultural and implementation arms. T2 is a calibrated middle path with a built-in evaluation gate at 24 months that mechanically escalates to T3 if the public-health gain is insufficient.

Quantitative anchor (bracketed estimate)

Using the shared parameter set and the Sri Lanka attributable-fraction bracket:

  • Terbufos attributable fraction of fatal SA pesticide poisonings: 20–35 % (point ~25 %), anchored to Sri Lanka single-agent withdrawal studies and consistent with Davies 2023 paediatric SA dominance.1516
  • Applied to ~1,500 fatal pesticide-poisoning deaths/yr → ~300–500 deaths/yr are plausibly terbufos-attributable.
  • Option T3 (full withdrawal) at Sri Lanka-class substitution-incompleteness (60–80 % of attributable burden actually retired) → ~200–400 deaths/yr averted.
  • Option T2 (formulation restriction + RUP + audit), partial coverage of the informal-market diversion pathway only → ~80–180 deaths/yr averted, with the 24-month evaluation gate sized to detect this signal.

This is a bracketed working estimate, not a point claim. The bracket is wide on purpose. The single piece of evidence that would tighten it — a live-patient adult attributable fraction — is the Surveillance Option S3 deliverable at month 12. Decision-makers should not wait for it: the lower end of the bracket alone (~200 deaths/yr) at a regulatory cost of ~R1–3M/yr gives a leverage ratio of approximately 1 : 500 on household-borne mortality cost — the case for action is robust across the full bracket.

8. Recommended next step

Adopt Option T2 immediately as an interim measure, with a hard-coded 24-month evaluation gate that escalates to Option T3 unless a pre-agreed reduction in attributable serious-poisoning cases is demonstrated.

Concretely:

  1. Within 3 months. DALRRD gazette the formulation restriction, RUP designation, distributor audit, and lockable-container requirement.
  2. Within 6 months. NICD operationalises the Coordination Option C3 NICD→DALRRD intelligence feed with quarterly terbufos-specific reporting.
  3. Within 12 months. Surveillance Option S3 sentinel live-patient chemical toxicology site operational, producing the attributable-fraction evidence base.
  4. Within 24 months. Joint NDoH–DALRRD review against pre-agreed reduction target → escalate to Option T3 if target missed, retain Option T2 if met.
  5. In parallel. Commission the four explicit [Evidence gap] work items (market volume, attributable fraction, yield-impact, substitute hazard) to retire the uncertainty that today prevents a clean cost–benefit calculation.

This is the option that takes the strongest public-health action immediately compatible with the evidence on hand, while building the evidence base needed to defend a full ban on its merits at the 24-month gate.

## 1. Policy question

**Should South Africa restrict the use of terbufos, or ban it outright as a registered agricultural pesticide?**

## 2. Public health problem

Terbufos is a **WHO Class Ia ("Extremely Hazardous")** organophosphate insecticide.[^who] It is acutely toxic via oral, dermal, and inhalation routes, with a published rat oral LD50 of **1.6–4.5 mg/kg**[^who][^epa] — placing it among the most acutely toxic active ingredients still in commercial use anywhere. It acts via irreversible cholinesterase inhibition; sub-gram doses are lethal in adults and very small quantities are lethal in children.

**South African evidence base.** Davies et al. (2023), reviewing paediatric pesticide deaths in South Africa, identified **terbufos and methamidophos as the dominant agents** in fatal childhood pesticide poisoning, with most deaths occurring after ingestion of granular formulations diverted into the informal "street-pesticide" market.[^davies] This is corroborated by NICD/NMC clinical case reports and by the public-health record of mass-poisoning events in 2022–2024 linked to informally-sold granular OP pesticides.[^nicd] A formal case series with attributed agent breakdown across NMC + NHLS + forensic toxicology has **not yet been compiled** and is the single most important evidence-gap item to retire (see [open items in the Parameters Hub](../parameters_hub.html#open-items--to-be-retired-into-the-csv), TBF-2).

The public-health case rests on three claims:

1. **High intrinsic toxicity** at sub-gram doses — settled science (WHO Class Ia; rat oral LD50 1.6–4.5 mg/kg).[^who][^epa]
2. **Disproportionate contribution to severe and fatal poisonings** — supported in paediatric SA data (Davies 2023);[^davies] adult attributable fraction is not yet quantified pending live-patient chemical toxicology (Surveillance Option S3).
3. **High substitutability for its primary use cases** (soil insect / stalk borer / nematode control in maize and other field crops) — registered substitutes exist (see §5), but **SA-specific yield-impact modelling is not yet done** (open item TBF-3).

### 2b. International natural-experiment evidence {#international-natural-experiment-evidence}

South Africa does not have to model terbufos withdrawal from first principles. Three relevant natural experiments have already been run:

- **Sri Lanka (1995–2011).** Sequential bans on WHO Class I organophosphates reduced **pesticide-suicide mortality by ~50 %** and **total suicide mortality by ~21 %**, *without* compensating method substitution.[^gunnell] Each banned single agent typically accounted for **20–40 %** of fatal pesticide poisonings in the period immediately before its withdrawal.
- **Bangladesh, Nepal, India.** Class Ia/Ib restrictions show the same direction and order of magnitude.
- **EU (2009) and US EPA (2006).** Terbufos was withdrawn (EU) or moved to RUP-only with granular formulations voluntarily cancelled (US). **No documented maize-yield collapse in any producer state.**

The Sri Lanka studies are the strongest available evidence that single-agent Class Ia withdrawal **does** retire most of that agent's attributable fatal burden, and **does not** redistribute it to substitute agents at the same lethality. SA paediatric data (Davies 2023) is independently consistent with terbufos sitting in the 20–40 % attributable bracket.

[^gunnell]: Gunnell D, Knipe D, Chang S-S, Pearson M, Konradsen F, Eddleston M et al. *Prevention of suicide with regulations aimed at restricting access to highly hazardous pesticides: a systematic review of the international evidence.* Lancet Glob Health 2017;5:e1026–e1037. Knipe DW et al. *Suicide prevention through means restriction: impact of the 2008–2011 pesticide restrictions on suicide in Sri Lanka.* PLOS One 2017.

[^who]: World Health Organization. *The WHO Recommended Classification of Pesticides by Hazard and Guidelines to Classification*, current edition. Terbufos is listed in Class Ia (Extremely Hazardous).

[^epa]: US Environmental Protection Agency. *Reregistration Eligibility Decision (RED): Terbufos.* EPA-738-R-06-006, September 2006. Rat acute oral LD50 1.6–4.5 mg/kg; rat acute dermal LD50 1.0–7.4 mg/kg.

[^davies]: Davies J et al. (2023). Paediatric pesticide deaths in South Africa; documents terbufos and methamidophos dominance among fatal cases. Cited in [glossary.qmd](../glossary.qmd#key-sources).

[^nicd]: NICD/NMC clinical case reports and public communications, 2022–2024, on mass-poisoning incidents linked to informally-sold granular organophosphate pesticides. Formal case-series compilation pending (open item TBF-2).

## 3. Status quo

{{< include _shared_parameters.qmd >}}

::: {.callout-note}
## What DALRRD action delivers — and to whom

DALRRD itself bears **~R0 of the R2.34B annual burden**. That cost is carried by NDoH (~R70M hospital), NHLS (~R10M lab), and most heavily by **households (~R2.26B in out-of-pocket costs and lost lifetime earnings)**. DALRRD nevertheless holds the only lever — registration, restriction, recall — that prevents the underlying exposure in the first place. A regulatory action borne by DALRRD (~R1–3M/year of additional enforcement and inspectorate capacity) therefore retires household-borne mortality cost at a leverage ratio of roughly **1 : 500**: one rand of regulatory spend averts five hundred rand of household income loss. For DALRRD this is not a cost — it is the single highest-impact use of regulatory authority on the SADC pesticide-poisoning portfolio.
:::

**Why act now?**

- 2022–2024 mass-poisoning events placed terbufos in the public and political spotlight,[^nicd] creating a window for regulatory action that may not recur.
- The EU removed terbufos from its approved list more than a decade ago; South Africa is an outlier among middle-income agricultural exporters in still permitting it.
- The [Surveillance](surveillance_policy.qmd) + [Coordination](coordination_policy.qmd) investments give DALRRD, for the first time, the institutional pathway to act on a single-product signal.

## 5. Policy options

### Option T1 — No change *(status quo)*

Terbufos remains registered under existing conditions. Relies on general pesticide-handling regulations and clinician/EHP-led NMC notifications to detect harm. **This is the implicit decision if no regulatory action is taken.**

### Option T2 — Tighter restriction *(★ recommended interim)*

A bundle of measures short of an outright ban:

- **Formulation restriction.** Withdraw granular formulations that visually resemble food/seed; allow only encapsulated or liquid formulations dispensed via licensed agricultural distributors.
- **Restricted-use product (RUP) designation.** Sales only to certified applicators against a logged transaction; no informal-market sales.
- **Mandatory point-of-sale labelling and lockable container requirement.**
- **Quarterly DALRRD audit** of distributor records, with NICD→DALRRD intelligence feed (see Coordination brief Option C3) flagging geographies of concern.
- **Sunset clause.** Conditional 24-month re-registration; if NICD-attributed serious-poisoning cases (from S3 toxicology, once operational) do not decline by a pre-agreed margin, automatic transition to Option T3.

Cost: principally regulatory and enforcement. `[Evidence gap: DALRRD inspectorate cost model for RUP enforcement.]` Implementation: 6–12 months.

### Option T3 — Full ban / phase-out

Cancel terbufos product registrations under the Fertilizers, Farm Feeds, Agricultural Remedies and Stock Remedies Act. Phase-out window (12–24 months) to allow substitute uptake and product clearance. Aligns South Africa with the EU position.

**Trade-offs to manage:**

- *Crop-yield impact on maize.* `[Evidence gap: SA-specific yield modelling under leading substitute actives; requires DALRRD + grain-industry collaboration.]`
- *Substitute toxicity profile.* Some substitutes (e.g. fipronil, certain neonicotinoids) carry their own ecological and human-health concerns. `[Evidence gap: SA-specific comparative hazard assessment.]`
- *Smallholder access.* Substitutes may be more expensive or harder to access at smallholder scale. `[Evidence gap: substitute pricing and accessibility.]`
- *Compliance / black-market risk.* A poorly-enforced ban can drive demand to unregulated supply. Mitigated by DALRRD inspectorate capacity (Coordination C3) and by Option T2 acting as a 24-month stepping stone.

Cost: regulatory cost low; market-transition cost concentrated on growers and distributors. `[Evidence gap.]` Implementation: 12–24 months.

**How to read the tree.** Public-health, agricultural, and implementation consequences are shown separately rather than collapsed into a single utility. T1 is best for agriculture and implementation but worst for public health. T3 is best for public health but loads risk onto the agricultural and implementation arms. T2 is a calibrated middle path with a **built-in evaluation gate** at 24 months that mechanically escalates to T3 if the public-health gain is insufficient.

### Quantitative anchor (bracketed estimate)

Using the shared parameter set and the Sri Lanka attributable-fraction bracket:

- **Terbufos attributable fraction of fatal SA pesticide poisonings: 20–35 % (point ~25 %)**, anchored to Sri Lanka single-agent withdrawal studies and consistent with Davies 2023 paediatric SA dominance.[^gunnell][^davies]
- Applied to ~1,500 fatal pesticide-poisoning deaths/yr → **~300–500 deaths/yr are plausibly terbufos-attributable.**
- Option T3 (full withdrawal) at Sri Lanka-class substitution-incompleteness (60–80 % of attributable burden actually retired) → **~200–400 deaths/yr averted.**
- Option T2 (formulation restriction + RUP + audit), partial coverage of the informal-market diversion pathway only → **~80–180 deaths/yr averted**, with the 24-month evaluation gate sized to detect this signal.

This is a **bracketed working estimate**, not a point claim. The bracket is wide on purpose. The single piece of evidence that would tighten it — a live-patient adult attributable fraction — is the [Surveillance Option S3](surveillance_policy.qmd) deliverable at month 12. Decision-makers should not wait for it: the lower end of the bracket alone (~200 deaths/yr) at a regulatory cost of ~R1–3M/yr gives a leverage ratio of approximately **1 : 500** on household-borne mortality cost — the case for action is robust across the full bracket.

| Issue | Status | Mitigation |
|:------|:-------|:-----------|
| Regulatory authority | DALRRD has full authority under the Act | None required |
| Pesticide registration cancellation process | Established administrative pathway | Standard DALRRD process |
| Attributable-fraction evidence | `[Evidence gap]` | Stand up Surveillance Option S3 at 1 sentinel site within 12 months |
| Yield-impact evidence | `[Evidence gap]` | Commission grain-industry / ARC modelling alongside Option T2 |
| Substitute hazard profile | `[Evidence gap]` | DALRRD comparative hazard review at registration |
| Smallholder access to substitutes | `[Evidence gap]` | Grower advisory + transition subsidy via DALRRD |
| Compliance / black-market risk | High under T3 if poorly enforced | T2 as a 24-month stepping stone; Coordination C3 intelligence feed |
| Stakeholder consultation | Required | DALRRD + grain industry + civil-society public-comment process |

**Institutional responsibilities.** DALRRD leads regulatory action. NDoH/NICD supplies the attributable-harm evidence base (via [Surveillance](surveillance_policy.qmd) S3 and [Coordination](coordination_policy.qmd) C3). DTIC and Agriculture sector bodies lead substitution support. Civil-society and grower organisations participate in formal consultation.

## 8. Recommended next step

**Adopt Option T2 immediately as an interim measure, with a hard-coded 24-month evaluation gate that escalates to Option T3 unless a pre-agreed reduction in attributable serious-poisoning cases is demonstrated.**

Concretely:

1. **Within 3 months.** DALRRD gazette the formulation restriction, RUP designation, distributor audit, and lockable-container requirement.
2. **Within 6 months.** NICD operationalises the [Coordination Option C3](coordination_policy.qmd) NICD→DALRRD intelligence feed with quarterly terbufos-specific reporting.
3. **Within 12 months.** [Surveillance Option S3](surveillance_policy.qmd) sentinel live-patient chemical toxicology site operational, producing the attributable-fraction evidence base.
4. **Within 24 months.** Joint NDoH–DALRRD review against pre-agreed reduction target → escalate to Option T3 if target missed, retain Option T2 if met.
5. **In parallel.** Commission the four explicit `[Evidence gap]` work items (market volume, attributable fraction, yield-impact, substitute hazard) to retire the uncertainty that today prevents a clean cost–benefit calculation.

This is the option that takes the strongest public-health action immediately compatible with the evidence on hand, while building the evidence base needed to defend a full ban on its merits at the 24-month gate.
